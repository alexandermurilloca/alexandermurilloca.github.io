# Cybersecurity of Québec's Elderly Population: Toward a Comprehensive Strategy

**By Marlon Alexander Murillo Medina**  
*MSc Cybersecurity — Université de Sherbrooke · Sherbrooke, Québec, Canada · 2026*

> 📄 Also available in: [🇫🇷 Français](cybersecurite-aines-quebec-fr) · [🇪🇸 Español](ciberseguridad-adultos-mayores-quebec-es)

## Abstract

This report analyses the issue of cybersecurity among elderly residents of Québec. Drawing on evidence-based data from the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre (CAFC) and Statistics Canada, the document establishes the disproportionate exposure of this cohort to phishing, financial fraud, and identity theft.

**Key findings:**
- Persons 60+ represent **40.3% of all national fraud losses** in Canada (2024)
- Average loss per victim: **$21,604** — highest across all age groups
- Age group 55–65: **lowest digital literacy scores** in all 3 assessed domains (PIAAC 2022)

**Keywords:** cybersecurity, elderly Quebec, online fraud, phishing, digital literacy, data protection, digital resilience, Loi 25.

---

## 1. Introduction

The contemporary digital era is radically transforming the way citizens interact with institutions and their social environment. In Québec, the protection of personal data and the digital security of older persons have become genuine imperatives of social justice and collective resilience in the face of technological crime.

Despite the existence of robust legislative frameworks such as *Loi 25*, current protection mechanisms appear insufficient to counter the growing ingenuity of cybercriminals targeting this cohort. This report examines the scope of digital threats and analyses the structural gaps in existing prevention measures.

---

## 2. Situational Overview: A Systemic Vulnerability

### 2.1 Scope and Evolution of Cyber Threats

Digital fraud has become a documented and rapidly expanding threat to older Canadians:

| Indicator | Key Data | Source |
|-----------|-----------|--------|
| Total fraud losses — 2021 | $383M nationally; 60+ submitted most reports | CAFC, 2024 |
| Losses — persons 60+ (2024) | **40.3% of national losses; avg. $21,604/victim** | CAFC, 2025 |
| Digital literacy — Canada 2022 | Age 55–65: lowest scores in all 3 assessed domains | Statistics Canada, 2024 |
| Primary cyber threat | Cybercrime; fraud and scams: most common forms | CCC, 2024 |

These schemes rely heavily on **social engineering** — psychological manipulation to extract confidential data. *Vishing* (voice phishing) and romance scams are particularly effective against elderly individuals whose trust in authority figures tends to be deeply ingrained.

### 2.2 Specific Vulnerability Factors

According to PIAAC 2022 results published by Statistics Canada in December 2024:

> Among all age groups in Canada, **the 55–65 age group obtained the lowest scores in all three assessed domains: literacy, numeracy, and adaptive problem solving** (Statistics Canada, 2024, p. 5).

Social isolation — accentuated by the COVID-19 pandemic — led many elderly people to intensify their use of digital technologies without adequate training, considerably increasing their risk exposure.

---

## 3. Limits of Current Protection Mechanisms

Three critical dimensions limit the effectiveness of current measures:

1. **Geographic disparity**: elderly in rural areas have significantly fewer training resources than urban counterparts
2. **Inadequacy of content**: awareness campaigns use complex technical jargon distributed exclusively through digital channels — excluding those they aim to protect
3. **Absence of synergy** between law enforcement, healthcare, and banking fragments interventions

---

## 4. Toward a Comprehensive Strategy

Three interdependent pillars:

**Axis 1 — Community-based training**: leverage existing CLSC networks to provide human, in-person support anchored in citizens' daily lives.

**Axis 2 — Legislative strengthening**: evolve *Loi 25* to impose enhanced vigilance obligations on financial institutions (OECD, 2020).

**Axis 3 — Québec Observatory**: create a multisectoral body coordinating law enforcement, private sector, and healthcare institutions.

---

## 5. Recommendations

### R1 — Institutionalize a National Digital Literacy Program
Create a provincial training program integrated into CLSC and public library networks with sustainable funding. In-person delivery with practical modules on social engineering recognition and secure digital identity management.

### R2 — Amend Loi 25 for Vulnerable Populations
Require financial institutions to implement enhanced vigilance obligations — including reinforced alert systems for atypical transactions involving clients aged 65 and over.

### R3 — Create a Québec Observatory for Elderly Cybersecurity
Multisectoral body bringing together government, law enforcement, the financial sector, and academia to centralize data and scientifically evaluate interventions.

---

## 6. Conclusion

The cybersecurity of elderly people must no longer be perceived as an individual responsibility, but as a pillar of Québec's digital sovereignty. As technology continues to redefine our interactions, one question remains: **will we be capable of building a digital environment where growing old no longer rhymes with becoming a target?**
